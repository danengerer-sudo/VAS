Work Zone Intrusion Alert

CLIENT Kevin Bryant PHASE 1 — approach DATE 14 SEP 2026 REV A
CONCEPT — FOR DISCUSSION, NOT A DELIVERABLE

Seven block layouts covering the deployment, the control architecture decision, what a sensing node actually does, how a hit is discriminated from a footstep, and the two findings that reshape the product — siren coverage and latency.

Numbers shown are first-cut engineering estimates, not calculated results. They exist to size the boxes and to show which quantities the design turns on. Several depend on Section 3 inputs Kevin has not supplied yet; those are listed at the end.

01Deployment

The line runs longitudinally along the work zone boundary, so an intruding vehicle crosses it at a shallow angle rather than perpendicular. Segmenting it is what makes the length open-ended: a longer zone is more segments, not a redesign.

02The architecture decision

Same four segments, same alert hardware, two ways to wire the middle. The difference is the cable plant and where the thinking happens.

03Inside a node

Two transducers per segment, one at each end of the hose. Firing on first arrival keeps latency down; the second arrival confirms the hit, locates it along the segment, and proves the hose is still intact.

04Detection logic

A single threshold forces one failure mode or the other. Three tiers separate the unambiguous case — fire now, no confirmation — from the band where a second opinion is worth the milliseconds it costs.

05Head end and the output path

One pair of conductors carries three states, the way a fire alarm initiating circuit has for decades. The output it drives contains no processor — which is a better safety argument than any amount of controller pedigree.

06Alert coverage

This is the finding most likely to change the product. A 120 dB @ 1 m siren — already a large horn — delivers roughly 90 dBA at 100 ft and 84 dBA at 200 ft in free field. Work zone background is 80–90 dBA, and the crew is in hearing protection.

07Latency budget

Transducers at both ends halve the worst case: a strike at the segment midpoint is 50 ft from a sensor, not 100. The siren's own rise time turns out to be the largest single term in the chain.

08What these drawings assume

Each figure rests on a Section 3 input the client owes us. Until they land, the boxes are right and the numbers are placeholders.

Open inputs, by figure

FIG. 1 — FIG. 2
Maximum deployment length of the sensing line, and the range of lane and work zone configurations it must cover. Segment count, bus loading and setup time all follow from it.
FIG. 3 — FIG. 4
Vehicle types and speeds that must trigger the system, and everything that must not. This list is the Validation Specification in draft form.
FIG. 4
The detection priority: miss-avoidance versus false-alarm-avoidance. The tier boundaries cannot be set without it, and it should be the client's call in writing.
FIG. 5
Expected service environment and product life, including the low-temperature limit. It decides component grade throughout.
FIG. 6
Required alert distance — how far from the line the crew must hear and see the alert. This one likely decides whether the product ships with one alert stack or several.
FIG. 7
Intended power source and any runtime requirement. Siren selection drives both the largest latency term and the peak current.
All figures
Target unit cost and annual volume, which decide how much of this collapses onto a custom node board in a later phase.
